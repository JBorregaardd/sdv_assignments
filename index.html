How have drug related incidents evolved in San Francisco since the early 2000s?

Recorded drug offenses in San Francisco changed in two ways over the last two decades. The total number of incidents dropped sharply after the late 2000s, but the pattern also became less evenly spread across the city and more concentrated in a single central hotspot in the Tenderloin area.

By s181487, s260126, s234667
Introduction

Looking at drug offenses in San Francisco

This story uses publicly available incident reports from the San Francisco Police Department covering the years 2003 to 2025. Each record describes a reported incident, including when it happened, where it happened, and what type of offense was registered. Here, the focus is on drug-related incidents such as possession, sale, and distribution of drugs [DataSF].

We use these records to examine two questions: how the number of reported drug incidents changed over time, and how those incidents were distributed across the city. It is important to keep in mind that these are recorded incidents, so they do not directly measure actual drug activity, but also reflect enforcement and reporting patterns.

Trend over time

The number of recorded drug offenses drops after the late 2000s

Drug offenses are high in the early and late 2000s, reaching a peak around 2009. After that, the number falls sharply through the 2010s and remains well below the earlier high. By 2021, the number of recorded incidents is much lower than at the peak. In the most recent years, the trend turns upward again. Overall, the figure suggests a long-term decline in recorded drug incidents, interrupted by a modest recent increase.

The decline in recorded drug offenses could be related to several factors, including changes in enforcement, shifts in drug markets, or broader social and economic developments. Over the same period, San Francisco has experienced significant economic growth, particularly driven by the tech boom, which has reshaped the city and contributed to increasing inequality (Brookings Institution, 2018). While these changes provide important context, the data alone does not allow us to determine the specific causes of the decline.

Spatial distribution

Hotspots persist in the city center while others disappear over time

The animated heatmap below shows the spatial distribution of recorded drug offenses across San Francisco over time, with the intensity indicating how concentrated incidents are in different areas of the city.

Drug offenses are not just unevenly distributed, but highly concentrated in a few specific areas. The strongest and most persistent hotspot is located in the Tenderloin and nearby parts of the city center, where high levels of recorded incidents remain visible throughout the entire period.

In contrast, earlier in the data there is also a noticeable hotspot in the Bayview–Hunters Point area in the southeast of the city. Over time, this hotspot becomes weaker and is almost gone by the late 2010s. This suggests that while the overall number of incidents declines, the spatial pattern also changes, with activity becoming more concentrated in fewer locations.

One possible explanation for the disappearance of the Bayview–Hunters Point hotspot is the broader development of the area over time. The neighborhood has undergone significant changes, including redevelopment and demographic shifts, which may have influenced where drug-related incidents are recorded (Bayview–Hunters Point, Wikipedia). At the same time, the continued concentration in the Tenderloin should be interpreted with caution. It may reflect both the visibility of drug activity and a stronger focus of enforcement in central parts of the city (San Francisco Police Commission, 2023).

Concentration over time

A growing share of incidents is concentrated in a few small areas

The final figure looks more directly at concentration. Instead of showing where incidents happen on a map, it divides the city into small grid cells of roughly 300 by 300 meters and shows how large a share of all recorded drug offenses falls within the top k most active cells each year.

The pattern is clear: over time, a larger share of incidents is captured by fewer locations. In 2003, the single most active cell accounts for 8.4% of all recorded drug offenses, while in 2021 it accounts for about 25%. Looking more broadly, the top 20 cells contain around 59% of all incidents in 2003, compared with 76% in 2025. This supports the pattern seen in the heatmap, where drug offenses become increasingly concentrated in a smaller number of hotspots over time.

Fewer incidents, but more concentrated

Taken together, the figures point to two main developments in recorded drug incidents in San Francisco. First, the total number of incidents rises into the late 2000s, then falls sharply through the 2010s, before increasing again in the most recent years. Second, the remaining incidents become increasingly concentrated in and around the Tenderloin, while an earlier hotspot in Bayview–Hunters Point becomes weaker over time.

This means the story is not simply that there are fewer recorded incidents overall. It is also that the spatial pattern changes: incidents become less spread across the city and more tightly clustered in a small number of central locations. Because the data comes from police incident reports, these patterns should be interpreted as patterns in recorded and enforced incidents, not as direct measures of underlying drug activity.

Notes

Notes on data and interpretation

Method note. The analysis uses San Francisco Police Department incident-report datasets: a historical dataset covering 2003–2025. Because these records come from different city data systems, long-run comparisons should be interpreted with some caution, especially around the transition period.

Interpretation note. These data reflect recorded police incidents, not direct measures of actual drug activity. Patterns in the figures may therefore capture changes in reporting and enforcement as well as changes in underlying activity.
