Drug offenses fell in San Francisco, but the pattern narrowed into hotspots

Recorded drug offenses in San Francisco changed in two ways over the last two decades. The total number of incidents dropped sharply after the late 2000s, but the pattern also became less evenly spread across the city and more concentrated in a small number of hotspots.

Introduction

How drug offense patterns changed over time

This analysis looks at recorded drug offenses in San Francisco from 2003 to 2025. The total number of incidents rises into the late 2000s, then falls strongly throughout the 2010s, and finally turns upward again in recent years. At the same time, the spatial pattern changes: as the number of incidents falls, the remaining incidents become more concentrated in fewer locations rather than disappearing evenly across the city.

These data reflect police-recorded incidents and therefore capture not only underlying activity, but also enforcement and reporting patterns. Still, they provide a useful view of how the geography of recorded drug offenses has changed over time.

Trend over time

The number of recorded drug offenses drops after the late 2000s

Drug offenses are high in the early and late 2000s, reaching a peak around 2009, and then fall sharply through the 2010s. By 2021, the number of recorded incidents is much lower than at the peak. In the most recent years, the trend turns upward again.

This overall decline is substantial, but it does not describe how incidents are distributed across the city. While the total number decreases, the remaining incidents do not disappear evenly.

Spatial distribution

The decline is not evenly distributed across the city

As the total number of incidents falls, the spatial distribution shows a clear pattern. Instead of declining uniformly, incidents remain concentrated in a smaller number of central areas.

The strongest concentration appears around the Tenderloin and nearby parts of the city center, with additional clusters in areas such as Southern and Mission. The overall decline in incidents therefore coincides with increasing spatial concentration.

Interpretation note: hotspots reflect recorded incidents and may also capture enforcement and reporting patterns, not only underlying activity.
Concentration over time

A smaller number of locations account for a larger share of incidents

The final figure shows how much of the total number of incidents is captured by the most active locations. Over time, a larger share of incidents is concentrated in fewer places.

Even as the overall number of incidents declines, the concentration increases, reinforcing the pattern observed in the map.

Conclusion

Fewer incidents, but more concentrated

The total number of recorded drug offenses falls after the late 2000s, but the remaining incidents become increasingly concentrated in a smaller number of locations.

This highlights a shift in the spatial structure of recorded incidents: fewer overall, but less evenly distributed.

Data source: San Francisco Police Department incident reports.
